### 1. The Surveillance-Pricing Teardown

> **Every Shopify store running a personalization app is about to be sitting on a $12,500-per-violation problem — and almost none of them know the app is doing it.**

**Source:** https://carbonlg.com/california-business-law-changes-2026/ — Carbon Law Group. A compliance watch-list for $1M–$20M California businesses, flagging AB 2564 (surveillance pricing ban) and AB 1776 (antitrust expansion) as the two changes mid-market operators are least prepared for.

California's AB 2564 would prohibit retailers from setting customized prices using personally identifiable information gathered through electronic surveillance, with carve-outs for transparent, universally-available loyalty discounts. Supporting research confirms the enforcement teeth: civil penalties up to **$12,500 per violation**, tripled for intentional conduct, brought by public prosecutors, with consumers able to seek injunctive relief *and attorney's fees*. The catch that makes this a business: most small merchants have no idea what their installed apps do. Dynamic-pricing plugins, cart-abandonment discounters, geo-pricing tools, and "smart" loyalty tiers were all bought as growth levers, and any of them may be pricing off personal data. Nobody has audited them.

⚠️ **Timing caveat — read this before you build:** AB 2564 was still moving through the legislature as of mid-2026 (passed the Assembly May 27, 2026; the governor's signing deadline is **September 30, 2026**). It is *not* yet settled law. That is the opportunity and the risk: build the offer now, sell it the week it's signed. If it's vetoed, the same teardown re-sells against New York and other states' analogous bills, which are moving on similar lines.

**Snapshot**
- ◆ **Initial investment:** $0–$400 (a domain, a Google Workspace seat, one paid legal-tracker subscription)
- ⏱️ **Time to first revenue:** 2–4 weeks after the bill is signed; 4–6 weeks if you pre-sell audits now at a discount
- ⌛ **Ongoing effort:** 6–10 hrs/week; each audit is roughly 3–5 hours of work
- ⬆ **Return potential:** $750–$2,500 per teardown; 3 per month is $2,250–$7,500/mo at near-100% margin
- ⟳ **Model:** One-off productized service, with a natural $150–$300/mo "monitoring" recurring upsell
- ⚙ **Need to start:** Ability to read app permissions and privacy policies, a screen recorder, a written report template. No law license — you are documenting what software does, not giving legal advice.
- ⚖️ **Watch-outs:** Do **not** offer legal opinions or say "you are compliant." Sell a *factual inventory*: here is every tool touching price, here is the data each one ingests, here are the ones your attorney should look at. Partner with one small business attorney for referrals in both directions.
- ◎ **Target customer:** California DTC brands doing $1M–$20M — big enough to have a personalization stack, too small to have in-house counsel
- ⚡ **Speed to cash:** High — deadline-driven urgency does the selling for you

**First steps**
1. Build the inventory checklist this weekend. Open a free Shopify dev store, install the 20 most popular pricing, loyalty, and personalization apps, and document exactly what data each requests and whether it can vary price per-shopper. That artifact *is* your product's engine and it doubles as your marketing.
2. Turn it into a free lead magnet: "The 20 Shopify Apps That May Break California's New Pricing Law." Publish it, and gate the full per-app breakdown behind an email address.
3. Write the deliverable template — 6–10 pages: tool inventory, data-flow-per-tool, a red/yellow/green triage, and a "questions for your attorney" page. Templating this once is what makes each audit a 3-hour job.
4. Find one small-business attorney doing CA privacy or consumer work. Offer to do their next three client audits at cost in exchange for referrals. Their credibility closes deals you can't.
5. Pre-sell at half price now, contingent on signature — "$500 if it's signed by Sep 30, refunded if not." You get paid to build a pipeline and learn whether anyone actually cares before you've spent a weekend more.
6. On signing day, email every list you've built within 24 hours. Being first in the inbox on the day it becomes news is worth more than being the best on the topic a month later.

### 2. Cannabis Café Opening Kit

> **San Francisco just created a permit category with a fixed, countable, hand-raised customer list — and every one of them has to solve the same four operational problems in the same six months.**

**Source:** https://www.kqed.org/news/12091247/san-francisco-approves-cannabis-cafes-a-first-for-the-bay-area — KQED. SF's Board of Supervisors approved cannabis cafés 7–4, a Bay Area first, implementing 2024's state law AB 1775.

The specifics make this unusually tractable. Operators need **two** permits (Office of Cannabis + a Department of Public Health consumption permit), must meet **full restaurant food-safety standards**, must run **electronic age verification at the door**, may serve food and non-alcoholic drinks, and may not allow anything to leave the building or sell alcohol or tobacco. Crucially, for **year one only, applications are limited to existing SF storefront retailers and their equity partners** — a small, public, enumerable list. Supporting research indicates SF's fee structure runs a **$2,000 nonrefundable application fee plus a $3,000 first-year license fee**, which tells you these operators are already spending real money and will pay to de-risk it. And here's the mismatch you sell into: these are *retail* operators. They have never run a kitchen, never trained a food handler, never passed a health inspection. The city handed them a restaurant problem and gave them a year to solve it.

**Snapshot**
- ◆ **Initial investment:** $200–$800 (a California Food Safety Manager certification runs ~$130–$200 and is the credential that makes you credible; the rest is document design)
- ⏱️ **Time to first revenue:** 3–6 weeks
- ⌛ **Ongoing effort:** 8–12 hrs/week
- ⬆ **Return potential:** $1,500–$5,000 per operator for the kit-plus-walkthrough; a realistic 5–10 operators in year one is $7,500–$50,000, plus ongoing retainers
- ⟳ **Model:** Productized consulting + a reusable template pack (build once, sell many)
- ⚙ **Need to start:** Food Safety Manager cert, a careful read of the ordinance and DPH consumption-permit requirements, document templates
- ▣ **Total addressable customers:** Small and finite — this is a feature. You can personally reach every eligible operator in SF in one afternoon.
- ⚖️ **Watch-outs:** Cannabis remains federally illegal — expect banking friction, and get paid by ACH or check, not by card processors that may claw back. Don't touch the plant, don't handle product, don't take equity in a licensee. You are selling operational documents and training.
- ⌂ **Online vs local:** Local first, then licensable — every other California city that adopts AB 1775 needs the identical kit
- ▲ **Scalability:** The SF kit becomes the Oakland kit becomes the LA kit with a find-and-replace and one afternoon of ordinance diffing

**First steps**
1. Read the ordinance and the DPH consumption-permit requirements end to end, and build a single one-page checklist of every operational obligation. Nobody in this industry has that page yet. It is your entire opening pitch.
2. Get the California Food Safety Manager certification this month. It's ~$130–$200, takes a weekend, and it's the difference between "consultant" and "guy with a checklist."
3. Pull the public list of SF permitted storefront cannabis retailers from SF.gov. That is your complete year-one prospect list, and it's probably a few dozen names.
4. Build the kit: employee food-handler training outline, a HACCP-style food-safety plan template, an electronic age-verification vendor comparison (three options, with pricing), the no-product-leaves-the-building SOP, and a mock health-inspection walkthrough.
5. Walk into five dispensaries in person with the one-page checklist printed. In-person beats cold email by an enormous margin in this industry, and you'll learn the real objections in an afternoon.
6. Price it as a tiered offer: $1,500 for the document kit alone, $3,500 with a two-hour on-site walkthrough and staff training, $500/mo to keep the documents current as DPH issues guidance.

### 3. The Token Bill Audit

> **Peer-reviewed research says most agent workloads are being run on models 10x more expensive than they need — you keep 30% of what you save, and the client pays you nothing if you find nothing.**

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02153 — arXiv, "Small Language Models are the Future of Agentic AI." Argues that agentic systems mostly perform "a small number of specialized tasks repetitively and with little variation," making small language models sufficiently capable, better suited, and necessarily more economical; the authors propose an LLM-to-SLM conversion algorithm and heterogeneous multi-model systems.

The paper hands you an *authoritative citation* for something engineers already suspect but can't get budget to prove. Supporting research puts hard numbers on it: fine-tuned small models can match larger ones on narrow tasks at **5–20x less per token**; prompt caching saves ~90% on cached reads; model routing yields 40–70%; batch processing costs 50% less than realtime. Real-world reports land in the **60–85%** reduction range — one documented case cut $4,200/mo to $1,130/mo, a 73% drop, with no perceived quality loss. Meanwhile every seed-stage startup has an AI bill that grew faster than anyone modeled and no engineer with a free week to attack it. You are selling found money, which is the easiest thing on earth to sell.

**Snapshot**
- ◆ **Initial investment:** $100–$500 (API credits for benchmarking; open-source eval tooling is free)
- ⏱️ **Time to first revenue:** 2–3 weeks
- ⌛ **Ongoing effort:** 10–15 hrs/week; each engagement is 15–25 hours
- ⬆ **Return potential:** On a $10k/mo bill, a 60% cut saves $72k/year; at 30% of first-year savings that's ~$21k from one client. Even a flat $3,000–$6,000 per audit is strong for the hours.
- ⟳ **Model:** Performance-based one-off, with an optional $500–$1,500/mo routing-maintenance retainer
- ⚙ **Need to start:** Working knowledge of LLM APIs, ability to build a task-specific eval set, familiarity with a routing library and at least one small-model host
- ✎ **Skill level:** Intermediate engineer — you must be able to prove quality *didn't* drop, which is the whole job
- ⚖️ **Watch-outs:** Never touch production traffic. Work against a mirrored eval set and hand back a PR. Get an NDA and be explicit that you don't retain prompt data — you'll be looking at their crown jewels.
- ⚡ **Speed to cash:** Contingency pricing kills the procurement conversation entirely — there's no budget approval needed for "free unless it works"

**First steps**
1. Build the eval harness first, not the optimizations. Take a public agent benchmark, wire up 4–5 models across price tiers, and produce a quality-vs-cost curve. Everything you sell rests on being able to say "quality held" with receipts.
2. Do it to yourself, publicly. Take one open-source agent project, cut its token cost 60%+, and write the teardown with real before/after numbers and the eval to prove parity. This post is your entire sales funnel.
3. Package a free 30-minute "bill triage": they share a usage export, you return a one-page estimate of the savings available. Costs you half an hour, and converts because the number is concrete.
4. Write the standard audit deliverable: per-endpoint cost breakdown, the three highest-spend call paths, a proposed routing table, caching opportunities, batch candidates, and the eval results proving quality held.
5. Price it contingency-first — **30% of the first year's verified savings, nothing if savings are under 20%.** The risk reversal is what gets you in the door with no track record.
6. Hunt where the bills are visible: YC company job posts mentioning inference cost, engineering blog posts about scaling AI features, and anyone complaining about their API bill on Hacker News this month.

### 4. The Missing Urine Test

> **Europe's cardiologists were just told to test every heart patient's kidneys — and existing data shows two-thirds of those patients have never had the test ordered.**

**Source:** https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260902234447.htm — ScienceDaily, covering new European Society of Cardiology / European Renal Association guidelines published in the *European Heart Journal* and presented at ESC Congress 2026.

The guidelines recommend testing **every patient with cardiovascular disease for chronic kidney disease at the time of CVD diagnosis** — eGFR from blood, plus urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) — organized under a "STAMP on CKD" framework (Screen, Triage, Address CKD risk, Modify CVD management, Plan health services). Roughly 100 million people in Europe have CKD. The commercial insight is the size of the gap between the guideline and reality: supporting research shows uACR testing rates of only **35.1%** among diabetes patients and a startling **4.1%** among hypertension patients in an international cohort of 3 million; one Swedish study found only **23%** of patients meeting CKD criteria had the diagnosis recorded in their chart at all. A new guideline plus a documented 60-point implementation gap is exactly the condition under which clinics buy tools — and under which pharma companies making SGLT2 inhibitors and RAS inhibitors (both named in the guidance) fund education.

**Snapshot**
- ◆ **Initial investment:** $300–$1,200 (medical illustration, a Ghost or Beehiiv subscription, one CME-adjacent design pass)
- ⏱️ **Time to first revenue:** 6–10 weeks — slower than the others; clinical audiences take longer to trust you
- ⌛ **Ongoing effort:** 6–10 hrs/week
- ⬆ **Return potential:** Modest but durable. $2k–$8k per sponsored placement to a niche clinical list; $99–$299 per clinic for a screening-workflow pack.
- ⟳ **Model:** Content/audience play converting to sponsorship and small digital products
- ⚙ **Need to start:** Clinical literacy (nursing, pharmacy, or med-adjacent background strongly preferred), careful sourcing discipline
- ⚖️ **Watch-outs:** This is the highest-credibility-barrier idea here. **Never give clinical advice** — you summarize published guidelines and cite them. Disclose any pharma sponsorship prominently. If you have no clinical background, partner with a nephrologist or cardiology NP and split revenue; without that, skip this one.
- ✎ **Skill level:** High domain trust required, low technical skill
- ▲ **Scalability:** Low ceiling, but very defensible — narrow clinical audiences are hard to build and easy to keep

**First steps**
1. Read the actual *European Heart Journal* guideline document, not the press coverage. Your entire value is being the person who read the primary source, and the ScienceDaily summary is not it.
2. Build one genuinely excellent free asset: a one-page STAMP-on-CKD screening flowchart — which patient, which test, which threshold, what to do next — designed well enough to be printed and pinned in a clinic. Give it away with no gate.
3. Post it where the audience already argues about this: cardiology and nephrology professional forums, LinkedIn, and the relevant Twitter/X and Bluesky clinical communities. Ask for correction, publicly. Being visibly corrected by specialists builds more trust than being right alone.
4. Start a tight weekly digest — CVD/CKD overlap evidence, 400 words, one guideline or study per issue, every source linked. Do not chase volume; 1,500 nephrologists and cardiologists is a more valuable list than 50,000 general readers.
5. At around 1,000 verified clinical subscribers, approach two categories of sponsor: point-of-care uACR test manufacturers, and the medical-affairs teams behind SGLT2/RAS-inhibitor products. Both have education budgets and both are named in the guideline.
6. Package the paid product alongside it: a clinic implementation pack — EHR order-set language, a patient explainer handout, a staff huddle script — at $99–$299. Practices buy workflow, not information.

### 5. AI Disclosure Insurance for Agencies

> **The FTC's per-violation penalty is now $53,088, every undisclosed post counts separately, and the small agencies generating AI content at scale have no idea they're the ones holding the liability.**

**Source:** https://natlawreview.com/article/californias-ongoing-ai-regulation-key-deadlines-arriving-2026-and-beyond — The National Law Review. Details California AI Transparency Act (SB 942, amended by AB 853) obligations that became operative **August 2, 2026**, with $5,000-per-violation penalties counted per day, and further deadlines for hosting platforms and large online platforms in January 2027.

CAITA's direct obligations mostly bind "covered providers" with over a million monthly users — so the model companies, not your clients. But it is the visible edge of a much broader wave, and the broader wave is where the money is. Supporting research shows the FTC raised its maximum civil penalty to **$53,088 per violation in January 2026** and can treat undisclosed AI in an ad as a deceptive practice anywhere in the US, with each non-disclosed post counting separately — a 100-post campaign can theoretically exceed $5 million in exposure. New York's synthetic-performer disclosure law has been live since **June 9, 2026** ($1,000 first violation, $5,000 after). The practical compliance bar now includes a documented tool inventory, written risk classification, correct disclosure placement, EU AI Act metadata, state-specific variants, platform labels, and an editorial workflow that catches gaps pre-publication. Boutique agencies and creator shops are producing AI content at volume with none of that — and their client contracts usually make *them* the indemnifying party.

**Snapshot**
- ◆ **Initial investment:** $0–$300 (documentation and a landing page; the research is the product)
- ⏱️ **Time to first revenue:** 2–4 weeks
- ⌛ **Ongoing effort:** 5–8 hrs/week
- ⬆ **Return potential:** $1,000–$3,000 per agency setup, plus $200–$500/mo per agency to keep the matrix current. Ten agencies on retainer is $2,000–$5,000/mo for maybe a day of monthly work.
- ⟳ **Model:** One-off setup converting to recurring — the recurring piece is the real business
- ⚙ **Need to start:** Diligence, a spreadsheet, and the willingness to re-read regulations monthly. No technical or legal credential required.
- ⚖️ **Watch-outs:** Same boundary as idea #1 — you sell *documentation and workflow*, not legal advice. Say so in writing on every deliverable. The disclosure landscape genuinely shifts month to month, which is precisely why the retainer sells.
- ◎ **Target customer:** 5–30 person marketing agencies, influencer shops, and content studios shipping AI-assisted work for clients
- ▲ **Scalability:** Excellent — the compliance matrix is built once and licensed repeatedly, and every new state law makes the last version obsolete for everyone but you

**First steps**
1. Build the disclosure matrix this week: rows are jurisdictions (FTC, California, New York, EU AI Act, plus each major platform's own policy), columns are content type (image, video, audio, synthetic endorser, text). Fill every cell with the actual required disclosure language and placement. This spreadsheet is the whole business.
2. Publish the summary version free as "The 2026 AI Disclosure Matrix." Gate the full cell-by-cell language behind an email. Agency owners will forward it internally, which is exactly the distribution you want.
3. Write the setup deliverable: an AI tool inventory template, a risk-classification rubric, copy-paste disclosure language per platform, and a pre-publication editorial checklist that a junior coordinator can actually run.
4. Price it $1,500 for setup plus $300/mo for quarterly matrix updates and an alert whenever a new state law lands. Lead the pitch with the FTC's $53,088-per-violation number — it does the persuading.
5. Prospect where the exposure is highest: agencies publicly advertising "AI-powered content at scale." Their own marketing tells you they're generating volume, and volume is what turns a single mistake into a per-post multiplier.
6. Offer a free 15-minute "exposure check" — you review five of their recent client posts against the matrix and report what's missing. Finding two gaps in fifteen minutes closes the deal on the call.
